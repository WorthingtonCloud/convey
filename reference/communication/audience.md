# Audience: who it's for and how they'll see it

The brief lives in `brief.json`. Fill what the supplied material already answers. Ask only about real gaps.

## The audience's job

Before any slide, settle four things:

- What is going on that these people care about?
- What do they already know, and what might they have wrong?
- What should they understand, decide, or do afterward?
- What evidence would convince them, and what would change their mind?

Jargon is fine only when the audience already uses it. Keep status, impact, risk, and ask as separate ideas so none hides inside another.

## The five modes

A deck can serve more than one mode. Name the primary one and design for it first.

| Mode | Leads with | Density | Where detail goes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Live | One clear takeaway, with brief context if needed | Light. The speaker carries the words | Speaker notes |
| Read-ahead | A summary that stands on its own | Higher. Definitions, numbers, caveats, and sources on the slide | Optional appendix |
| Decision | The answer, the options, the tradeoffs, and the specific ask | Moderate. Criteria, costs, and unknowns shown openly | Assumptions, dissent, other scenarios |
| Operating review | Results against plan, and the exceptions | Moderate. Same metric definitions and windows every time | Segment and cause tables |
| Technical deep dive | The organizing model and the key design choices | High when the decision needs it. Overview first, then zoom in | Implementation detail |

Speaker notes help a live talk. They do not replace a caveat that a forwarded deck needs on the slide.

## Message, proof, so what, now what

Each section of the argument should carry four parts:

1. **Message**: the assertion, usually as the headline.
2. **Proof**: the evidence that supports it.
3. **So what**: why it matters to this audience.
4. **Now what**: the action or next step, if one is warranted.

Headlines should usually assert something. Section dividers, agendas, and reference diagrams can use labels. Avoid big verbs like "transformed" or "guaranteed" unless a claim backs them.

Expect two questions on every slide: "Against what baseline?" and "Where does that number come from?"

## Settled conflicts

Published guidance disagrees on several points. These are the rules this skill follows.

- **No universal word limit.** Readability depends on mode and viewing conditions. Check that the main point lands for a live viewer and that a read-ahead stands alone.
- **One principal question per slide, not one fact.** Several data marks can support a single question. A comparison matrix is fine when the decision needs things side by side.
- **No invented ask.** Every deck has an intended outcome. If no decision is needed, say "informational; no decision requested."
- **No cap on options.** Shortlist when you can, but include every option that is really viable.
- **Quantify only when reliable.** A number belongs when it is trustworthy and matters to the decision. A solid qualitative finding beats false precision.
